Prologue

Before the Ships

7th century to 1511

Scroll slowly

Long before Europe drew us on its maps, these islands were already a crossroads.

From Palembang, Srivijaya ruled the sea lanes. Its oldest dated inscription is from 682.

In 1293, Majapahit rose in East Java. Around 1336, its minister Gajah Mada swore the Palapa oath. He vowed to unite Nusantara, the islands beyond.

The oath survives only in later chronicles, so its exact words are uncertain.

And there was a secret the whole world wanted. Cloves grew only on a few islands in North Maluku. Nutmeg and mace grew only in Banda.

Tiny islands. Enormous desire. This is where our story begins.

Chapter I

The Cross and the Clove

Portugal and Spain, 1511 to 1663

In August 1511, Afonso de Albuquerque took Malacca. The gate to the spice islands was now in Portuguese hands.

A year later, Francisco Serrão was shipwrecked and brought to Ternate. The Portuguese stayed. In 1522 they began a stone fort there.

Trade turned into control. In 1570, the Portuguese murdered Sultan Hairun of Ternate.

His son answered.

Sultan Baabullah laid siege to the fort for five years. In 1575, the Portuguese left Ternate for good.

Spain came too. Magellan's surviving crew reached Tidore in 1521. Spain kept a foothold there until 1663.

Portugal held on in Timor much longer. Its rule there lasted until 1975.

They lost the islands. Their words stayed.

Chapter II

The Company

The VOC, 1596 to 1799

In June 1596, Dutch ships under Cornelis de Houtman anchored at Banten. Of the 249 men who sailed, only 87 made it home.

On 20 March 1602, the Dutch merged their rival trading firms into one. The Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie. The VOC.

It was a company that could sign treaties, build forts, raise soldiers and wage war.

On 30 May 1619, Jan Pieterszoon Coen destroyed Jayakarta. He built Batavia on its ashes.

In 1621, Coen came for Banda's nutmeg. Before the conquest, around 15,000 people lived there. Roughly 1,000 remained.

Population figures are historians' estimates. The collapse itself is not in doubt.

In 1667, peace was made at Breda. Each side kept what it held. So the Dutch kept the tiny nutmeg island of Run, and the English kept a place called Manhattan.

The Company grew rich. Then it grew rotten.

Its charter lapsed on 31 December 1799. The Dutch state took over its colonies and its enormous debts.

01 The Roadstead

Ships at anchor

Tall East Indiamen ride offshore. Small boats carry pepper, sugar, cloth and people. Not everyone on board came by choice.

Behind the harbour stands Kasteel Batavia, the Company's square fortress, built from 1620.

02 Kali Besar

A little Amsterdam

In 1631 and 1632 the river was straightened into a canal. Wealthy merchants built tall gabled houses along its banks.

There was one problem. Canals that suit Holland do not suit the tropics. The water silted. Mosquitoes bred.

03 Hoenderpasarbrug

The chicken market bridge

A wooden drawbridge was built here in 1628, near the chicken market. Rebuilt many times, it still stands as Jembatan Kota Intan.

Tap the bridge to raise it.

04 The Stadhuis

Where the city was judged

The City Hall was inaugurated in 1710. Courts sat upstairs. Cells lay beneath the front porch.

In 1830, Prince Diponegoro was held here before his exile. Today the building is the Jakarta History Museum.

05 Outside the walls

October 1740

Chinese settlers ran the sugar mills around the city. When the sugar trade fell, fear and rumour grew.

From 9 October 1740, at least 10,000 ethnic Chinese were killed in Batavia. Survivors were moved outside the walls, to Glodok.

06 Kota Tua

A graveyard, then a ghost

By the 1700s, Europeans called Batavia a graveyard. Malaria was the usual reason.

    Chapter III

    Iron Road, New Masters

    Daendels and Raffles, 1808 to 1816

    In 1808, Governor General Herman Willem Daendels arrived. His orders were to defend Java against the British.

    He drove a road across the island, from Anyer to Panarukan. About 1,000 kilometres in three years.

    0 km

    It was built with forced labour. One British account claimed about 12,000 deaths. Nobody kept an honest count.

    You may have driven on it. Much of it is the Pantura coast road today.

    In 1811 the British took Java. Thomas Stamford Raffles governed it until 1816.

    In 1814, Raffles sent an engineer to a hill near Magelang. Beneath the trees and ash lay Borobudur.

    In April 1815, Tambora exploded on Sumbawa. Tens of thousands died. The next year, Europe had no summer.

    In 1816, the British handed Java back to the Dutch. The masters changed. The road stayed.

    Chapter IV

    The Sweet and the Bitter

    War and the Cultivation System, 1825 to 1870

    In 1825, Prince Diponegoro rose against the Dutch. The Java War lasted five years. At least 200,000 Javanese died.

    On 28 March 1830 he came to Magelang to negotiate. He was arrested at the table, then exiled. He died in Makassar in 1855.

    That same year, Johannes van den Bosch brought the Cultivation System. Each village had to give a fifth of its land to export crops. Or 66 days of labour a year.

    In practice, it was often more. Famine struck Cirebon in 1843 and 1844, then Demak and Grobogan.

    Historians still debate how much drought and how much the System were to blame.

    The profits sailed north. By one estimate, around 840 million guilders between 1830 and 1870. At its peak, the colony paid about half of the Dutch state's revenue.

    In 1860, a former official named Eduard Douwes Dekker published Max Havelaar under the name Multatuli. The Netherlands could no longer say it did not know.

    Chapter V

    The Last Conquests

    Aceh, Bali and a debt of honour, 1873 to 1914

    The Dutch declared war on Aceh on 26 March 1873. Officially it ended in 1904. Guerrilla fighting went on for about another decade.

    Somewhere between 50,000 and 60,000 Acehnese died.

    On 20 September 1906, the court of Badung in Bali walked dressed in white into Dutch fire rather than surrender. This is the puputan, the ending.

    The Dutch finished conquering the archipelago only in the early 20th century. Remember that date. We will need it later.

    In 1899 a lawyer named Van Deventer wrote that the Netherlands owed the Indies "a debt of honour". In 1901 Queen Wilhelmina announced an Ethical Policy. Irrigation, education, emigration.

    It opened a few school doors.

    Through those doors walked the people who would end the colony.

    Chapter VI

    Awakening

    Letters, schools and a pledge, 1908 to 1928

    Kartini of Jepara wrote letters to Dutch friends about schooling, freedom and the lives of women. She died in 1904. She was 25.

    Her letters were published in 1911. In Malay, the title became Habis Gelap Terbitlah Terang. After darkness, light.

    On 20 May 1908, students at the STOVIA medical school founded Budi Utomo. Today, 20 May is National Awakening Day.

    In 1913, Soewardi Soerjaningrat wrote an essay called "If I Were a Dutchman". It got him exiled. He came home as Ki Hajar Dewantara and opened Taman Siswa schools in 1922.

    On 28 October 1928, young people from across the islands met in Batavia. They made three promises.

    Sumpah Pemuda. Tap each line to make the promise with them.

    That night, W.R. Supratman played a new song on his violin, without words. It was called Indonesia Raya.

    Chapter VII

    The Rising Sun

    The Japanese occupation, 1942 to 1945

    On 8 March 1942, the Dutch surrendered Java to Japan at Kalijati. After more than three centuries, the Dutch were gone in days.

    Some people welcomed the Japanese at first. The welcome did not last.

    Millions were forced to work as romusha. Estimates run from 4 to 10 million on Java. Many of those sent away never came home.

    Numbers vary widely between sources. All of them are terrible.

    Rice was taken for the army. Famine followed in 1944 and 1945.

    Japan also trained Indonesian soldiers in PETA from 1943, and let leaders plan a state. On 1 June 1945, Sukarno laid out Pancasila.

    On 15 August 1945, Japan surrendered.

    For a few days, nobody truly ruled. That gap was everything.

    Chapter VIII

    Merdeka

    Friday, 17 August 1945

    Before dawn on 16 August, young activists took Sukarno and Hatta to Rengasdengklok. They wanted a proclamation now. Not later.

    That night the text was drafted in Jakarta. Sayuti Melik typed it on a borrowed typewriter.

    P R O K L A M A S I

    Kami, bangsa Indonesia, dengan ini menjatakan kemerdekaan Indonesia.

    Hal-hal jang mengenai pemindahan kekoeasaan d.l.l., diselenggarakan dengan tjara saksama dan dalam tempo jang sesingkat-singkatnja.

    Djakarta, hari 17 boelan 8 tahoen 05

    Atas nama bangsa Indonesia,
    Soekarno/Hatta.

    We, the people of Indonesia, hereby declare the independence of Indonesia. Matters concerning the transfer of power will be carried out carefully and as quickly as possible.

    Notice the date. Tahoen 05 is the Japanese imperial year 2605. Even freedom was first dated in the occupier's calendar.

    At 10 in the morning, Tokyo time, at Jalan Pegangsaan Timur 56, Sukarno read it aloud. The flag was sewn by Fatmawati. Latief Hendraningrat and Suhud raised it.

    Chapter IX

    Four More Years

    The Revolution, 1945 to 1949

    Surabaya, 19 September 1945

    Tear the blue

    The Dutch came back and did not accept the proclamation. Above the Hotel Yamato, Dutch men raised their red, white and blue.

    Young Indonesians climbed to the roof. They tore off the blue strip. Red and white remained.

    Drag the blue strip down to tear it away.

    On 10 November 1945, British forces launched their main assault on Surabaya. Thousands of Indonesians died defending the city. Today, 10 November is Heroes' Day.

    Agreements came and broke. Linggadjati in 1947. Renville in 1948. Each was followed by a Dutch military offensive.

    On 1 March 1949, Republican forces held Yogyakarta for about six hours. It was short. The world noticed.

    At the Round Table Conference in The Hague, the Dutch agreed to leave. On 27 December 1949, sovereignty was transferred.

    The rest took longer to say.

    In 2020, the Dutch King apologised for excessive violence. In 2022, the Prime Minister apologised for extreme violence after a major study. In 2023, the Netherlands recognised 17 August 1945 as Indonesia's independence day.

    A myth worth breaking

    Was it really 350 years?

    You have heard it all your life. Dijajah 350 tahun. It is a powerful line. It is also not quite true.

    16001700180019001942

      Approximate years of effective Dutch control. Based on the conquest dates in this story. Historians draw these lines differently.

      The Dutch were present for about 350 years. They did not rule all of us for that long. In the 1960s the historian G.J. Resink showed that many kingdoms stayed independent under international law into the late 1800s and beyond.

      So here is a better line. Not 350 years of defeat. Centuries of resistance.

      Stone that stayed

      Buildings you can still visit

        i

        Rijsttafel

        The "rice table". A colonial banquet of many dishes, served with rice. Hotels like Des Indes in Batavia offered dozens at once.

        ii

        Kebaya

        European and Indo women adopted the kebaya, often edged with lace. In 2024, UNESCO listed it as shared heritage of five nations.

        iii

        Batik Belanda

        From about 1840 to 1940, Indo women ran batik workshops on the north coast. Tulips, bouquets and even Snow White appeared in wax.

        iv

        Kroncong

        Born among the Portuguese speaking Mardijkers of Kampung Tugu. Its small guitar descends from the Portuguese cavaquinho.

        v

        The railway

        The first line, Semarang to Tanggung, opened on 10 August 1867. It was built to move sugar. Today it moves you.

        vi

        Kretek

        Legend credits Haji Djamhari of Kudus, around 1880, who mixed cloves into tobacco. The name copies the crackle of burning cloves.

        The Quiet Colonization

        Colonies rarely end with a flag. The old questions are still here, wearing new clothes. Who owns the land? Who sets the price? Who keeps the records?
